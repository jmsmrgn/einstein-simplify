# Example: a spoken script segment

**Source** (a demo script line):

> Then, and this is where it gets interesting, each chunk gets converted into what's called a vector embedding. A vector is just a list of numbers, but it's a very specific list of numbers that represents the meaning of that chunk. The model that generates these vectors is trained to put semantically similar content close together in this numerical space.

**Invocation:** `/einstein-simplify for prospects at a product demo, mixed backgrounds; the next screen is labeled "Embeddings"`. **Purpose:** understand. **Medium:** spoken.

**Takeaway:** Our search finds pages by what they mean, not by the words they happen to use.

**New ideas (3):** word matching misses pages that say the same thing differently; each passage gets a position on a map where nearby means similar in meaning; a search places the question on the same map and returns what sits nearest.

**Output:**

> Here's the problem. Someone searches for "setting up the consent manager", but the page they need says "configuring consent mode". Same meaning, different words, and a search that matches words misses it.
>
> So we do something different. We give every passage a position on a map, and the position stands for what the passage means. Passages that mean the same thing land close together, even when they share no words. Those two consent pages end up side by side.
>
> When someone searches, we put their question on the same map and hand back whatever sits nearest. So the search finds pages by meaning, not by matching words.
>
> On the next screen you'll see these positions labeled "embeddings". That's what an embedding is: a passage's position on the map of meaning.
>
> So remember: we find pages by what they mean, not by the words they happen to use.

**Operator line:** Left out: that the map has hundreds of directions rather than two (nothing this audience does depends on it), and how the positions are learned.

**Why it looks like this:** the term is named only because the next screen shows it, and only after the idea exists. One image, the map, carries the whole segment. The takeaway lands once in the middle and again at the close. Sentences are short, with no parentheses or symbols to trip over when spoken. The analogy's limit is left out because this audience cannot act on it.
